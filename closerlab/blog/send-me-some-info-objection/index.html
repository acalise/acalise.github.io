"Just send me some info": the brush-off, handled

This is the most polite rejection in business, and reps fall for it constantly - because it feels like progress. You get to say "great, I'll send that over," you log it as a next step, and you never hear from them again. The material almost never gets read. But roughly one in ten of these is genuine, and the difference is findable with one question.

Quick Answer

Ask what should be in it. "Happy to - what should I make sure is in there? I'd rather send one relevant page than a deck you won't open." A genuine request produces a specific answer, because they actually want something. A brush-off produces vagueness like "just whatever you've got," which tells you politely that this is over. Either way you've learned more in ten seconds than you would from three weeks of follow-up.

Why it's usually a no

"Send me some info" is the ideal exit line and everyone knows it. It's polite, it avoids confrontation, it ends the call immediately, and it costs the buyer nothing. Nobody has ever been criticised for asking to see materials.

The tell is that it's a request for something unspecified. People who genuinely want information want particular information - a security overview, pricing structure, an integration list, a case study in their industry. "Whatever you've got" is not a request, it's a goodbye.

The qualifying question

Buyer"Why don't you send me some information and I'll take a look."

You"Happy to. What should I make sure is in there? I'd rather send you one relevant page than a deck you'll never open."

Three things make this work. It agrees, so there's no friction. It's self-deprecating about sales decks, which signals you're not going to spam them. And it asks a question they have to engage with in order to get rid of you - which is the whole point.

If it's vague, one more attempt

Buyer"Just send whatever you've got."

You"I'll do that. Can I ask honestly - is this something you'd actually want to look at, or is now just not the moment? Either answer is completely fine, I'd just rather know than chase you."

This works because it gives them permission to say no, which most buyers are relieved to be offered. You'll get one of three responses:

  • "Honestly, not right now." Excellent. You saved three weeks of follow-up and you can ask when to come back.
  • "No, I am interested, it's just busy." Now book something specific.
  • Awkward silence. Also an answer.

Reps hate this question because it invites a no. That's the feature. A fast no is worth more than a slow maybe - it costs you nothing and it clears your pipeline of things that were never real.

Always attach a next step

If you're sending something, never let it end with "I'll send it over."

You"I'll get that to you today. Rather than me chasing you - shall we put fifteen minutes in for Thursday? If the material's useless you can cancel it and I'll leave you alone."

A meeting booked while you're still on the call converts far better than one you try to arrange by email afterwards. And the escape clause makes it easy to say yes to.

What to actually send

Rules for the follow-up

One page, not a deck. Nobody opens a 30-slide attachment from a stranger.

Answer their exact question. If they asked about integrations, lead with integrations. Don't reformat your standard pitch.

Put it in the email body. Attachments create friction and land in spam filters. If it fits in five sentences, it goes in the email.

Reference the call specifically. "You mentioned the manual reconciliation step" beats "further to our conversation."

End with one question. An email with no question gets no reply.

How many times to follow up

If they went dark after asking for information, three to four attempts over two to three weeks is reasonable, each adding something rather than repeating. Then send a genuine close-out - "I'll assume the timing isn't right, I'll stop here, do reach out if that changes." Those get replies at a surprising rate, because they release the buyer from the obligation of avoiding you.

The upstream fix

If you hear this on most calls, the issue isn't your response to it - it's that you're reaching this point without having established anything. Buyers who feel a real problem being addressed don't ask for a PDF; they ask what happens next.

Look at the conversation before the brush-off. Did you find a problem they'd actually pay to remove? See discovery questions that surface real pain and the objection framework.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does it mean when a prospect says send me some info?

Around nine times out of ten it's a polite exit rather than a genuine request. It's the ideal brush-off because it's socially frictionless and ends the call immediately. The tell is specificity: someone who actually wants information asks for particular information, while someone who wants the call to end asks for "whatever you've got."

How do you respond to 'just send me some information'?

Agree, then ask what should be in it: "Happy to - what should I make sure is in there? I'd rather send one relevant page than a deck you'll never open." A specific answer means real interest and tells you exactly what to send. A vague answer means it's over, which is useful information you'd otherwise take three weeks of follow-up to discover.

Should you send information when a prospect asks for it?

Yes, but always attach a next step before you hang up - book a short call to discuss it, with an explicit option for them to cancel if the material isn't useful. Sending without a scheduled follow-up converts very poorly. Keep what you send to one page that answers their specific question, in the email body rather than as an attachment.

How many times should you follow up after sending information?

Three or four attempts over two to three weeks, each one adding something new rather than repeating the ask. Then send a clean close-out message saying you'll assume the timing isn't right and you'll stop. Those final emails get replies at a surprisingly high rate, because they release the prospect from the discomfort of continuing to ignore you.
