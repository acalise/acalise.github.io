The 6 best ScreenZen alternatives for iPhone

ScreenZen is one of the genuinely good free apps in this category. Delays before an app opens, intention prompts, open-count limits, no subscription pushing at you. The reason people search for alternatives isn't that it's bad - it's that it's gentle, and a gentle tool has a ceiling. Once you've learned to tap through the prompt without reading it, more of the same won't help. Here are six alternatives, ranked by how hard they actually hold.

Quick Answer

If ScreenZen stopped working, don't look for a nicer version of ScreenZen - look for a different mechanism. For a hard gate you can't reason with, FightMode locks your apps until you finish a 60-second boxing round. For physical separation, Brick uses an NFC tag you have to walk to. For laptop-and-phone together, Freedom syncs blocklists across devices. If you actually just want a paid version of what ScreenZen already does, Opal and Clearspace are the polished options - but be honest about whether the mechanism is the problem before you pay for a better-looking one.

What ScreenZen does well, and where it runs out

Worth being fair to it first, because a lot of people should stay on it:

  • It's actually free. No trial countdown, no crippled tier. Rare in this category.
  • The intention prompt is a good idea. Being made to type why you're opening Instagram catches a surprising number of autopilot opens.
  • It's configurable. Delay length, open limits, and per-app rules give you more control than Apple's built-in tools.

The ceiling is structural, not a bug: every rule ScreenZen enforces is a rule you can lift. The delay is a delay you sit through. The intention prompt accepts "idk" as an answer. The open limit has a settings screen. On day one that's plenty of friction. By month three your thumb has learned the whole sequence and executes it without involving you.

The tell

If you can't remember the last time a ScreenZen prompt actually made you close the app, the tool isn't failing - it's finished. You've adapted to it. That's the signal to change mechanism, not settings.

1. FightMode - a task instead of a delay

The reason a delay stops working is that waiting costs you nothing but time, and you have plenty of that when you're already reaching for your phone. FightMode replaces the wait with work: your apps stay locked until you win a 60-second boxing round, coach calling combos, AI scorecard checking that you threw them.

You can't sit through it and you can't type "idk" at it. Either you do the round or you don't get in. That removes the negotiation entirely, which is the whole point - the negotiation is the part you keep losing. And unlike a delay, the 60 seconds produce something: do it ten times a day for a month and you've accidentally trained.

2. Brick - the only real physical gate

Brick is an NFC tag you stick to a wall at home. Tap it to lock your chosen apps; the only way to unlock is to physically return and tap it again. Leave the house locked and there is no software argument to have.

It's the hardest block on this list for one reason software can't replicate: distance. You cannot tap a tag from a bar. The trade-offs are that you're buying hardware, you have to not lose it, and there will be a day you genuinely need Maps and the tag is at home. See software alternatives to Brick.

3. Freedom - if the laptop is half the problem

ScreenZen is phone-only. If your worst hours happen in a browser tab, no phone tool will fix your day. Freedom syncs blocklists across phone and desktop and offers a locked session mode that makes early quitting deliberately painful.

Best fit for people whose distraction is work-shaped - the tab you open "for one second" during a hard task. Details in the Freedom comparison.

4. Opal - the polished paid option

Opal is what ScreenZen looks like with a design budget: scheduled focus sessions, streaks, session stats, and a locked mode that raises the cost of quitting. If you respond well to streaks and dashboards, the gamification does real work.

Be honest with yourself about the reason you're switching, though. If ScreenZen failed because you tapped through it, Opal's sessions are also endable and you'll tap through those too. See the Opal comparison.

5. Clearspace - same idea, more polish

Clearspace occupies almost exactly ScreenZen's territory - pause, breathe, state your intention - with a nicer build and a subscription. It's a genuine upgrade in feel and a lateral move in mechanism.

Worth it if you liked the approach and want it done better. Not worth it if the approach itself stopped landing. Full Clearspace breakdown here.

6. Screen Time - the free floor, not a step up

Listed for completeness: Apple's built-in App Limits and Downtime are free and fine as a baseline, but they're softer than ScreenZen, not harder. The Ignore Limit button ends any conversation. If you're leaving ScreenZen because it was too easy to bypass, this is a step backwards. See Screen Time vs third-party blockers.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is ScreenZen actually free?

ScreenZen has a genuinely usable free tier, which is unusual in this category - most competitors gate the core behaviour behind a subscription after a trial. That's a real point in its favour and a reason to try it before paying for anything else. Check the current App Store listing for what's included, since free tiers get restructured over time.

Why did ScreenZen stop working for me?

Almost certainly habituation rather than anything breaking. ScreenZen's mechanisms - a delay, an intention prompt, an open limit - all rely on you cooperating with a rule you set yourself. That's effective while the prompt is novel and your attention is still on it. After a few months your thumb executes the whole sequence automatically. The fix isn't longer delays; it's a mechanism that doesn't depend on your cooperation in the moment.

What's the hardest app blocker for iPhone?

Brick is the hardest in the literal sense, because it uses physical distance - an NFC tag in your house can't be tapped from anywhere else. Among software-only options, exercise-to-unlock apps like FightMode are the firmest, because completing a task can't be waited out the way a timer can. No consumer iOS app can be truly unbypassable, since Apple always lets you delete it; the realistic goal is making bypass slower and more effortful than the impulse can survive.

Should I use ScreenZen and another blocker together?

It can work, and the sensible pairing is a soft tool on your borderline apps and a hard tool on the one or two that actually cost you hours. Running two hard blockers over the same apps usually backfires - the overlap creates constant friction on things you legitimately need, and people respond by disabling both. Pick one app you genuinely lose time to, gate that one hard, and leave the rest on a nudge.
