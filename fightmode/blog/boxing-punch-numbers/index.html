Boxing punch numbers 1-10, explained

Walk into any boxing gym and someone will shout "one-two!" at you before they tell you what that means. The numbering system is shorthand - it lets a coach call a four-punch combination in under a second, and it lets you drill without watching a demo every time. The first six numbers are near-universal. After that, gyms disagree, and pretending otherwise is how people end up confused. Here is the whole system, honestly labelled.

Quick Answer

In the standard system: 1 = jab, 2 = cross, 3 = lead hook, 4 = rear hook, 5 = lead uppercut, 6 = rear uppercut. Those six are consistent almost everywhere. Numbers 7 through 10 are not standardised - most commonly they repeat 3-6 as body shots (7 = lead hook to the body, 8 = rear hook to the body, 9 = lead uppercut to the body, 10 = rear uppercut to the body), but some gyms use "b" suffixes instead (3b for a body hook) and never go past 6. Learn 1-6 cold, then ask your coach what they mean by 7.

The numbers, 1 through 6

These are the ones you can rely on. All descriptions assume an orthodox stance (left foot forward, left hand lead). If you're southpaw, "lead" is your right and "rear" is your left - the numbers don't change, the hands do.

1 - the jab

Lead hand, straight out, rotating so the palm faces down at impact. It's the most thrown punch in boxing by an enormous margin, and it's not primarily a damage punch - it measures distance, disrupts rhythm, and buys the half-second the rest of the combination needs. The jab returns to your cheek, not to your hip.

2 - the cross

Rear hand, straight down the centre, with the rear foot pivoting and the hips rotating into it. The power comes from the ground and the turn, not the arm. If your rear heel doesn't move, you're arm-punching.

3 - the lead hook

Lead hand travelling in a horizontal arc, elbow bent at roughly ninety degrees, driven by pivoting the lead foot and turning the torso. Short range - if you have to reach, it's the wrong punch. The most common beginner error is swinging it wide, which telegraphs it and drains the power.

4 - the rear hook

Same shape from the back hand. More power available, and more risk: it travels further, so it's slower to land and slower to recover from. Thrown carelessly it leaves your chin exposed for the whole trip.

5 and 6 - the uppercuts

Lead and rear hands driving upward through the centre. Uppercuts are close-range punches that exploit a high, tight guard - they slip between the gloves where hooks can't. The power is a leg drive, not an arm lift. Dropping your hand to load an uppercut is the classic tell, and it's what gets people countered.

Southpaw note

The numbers describe lead and rear, not left and right. A southpaw's 1 is a right jab and their 2 is a left cross. Mixed-stance gyms sometimes call punches by hand instead to avoid confusion - if a coach shouts "left hook" rather than "3", that's why.

Numbers 7 to 10: where gyms disagree

This is the part most articles state with false confidence. There is no governing body for punch numbering. Three conventions are common:

  • Body-shot extension (most common). 7 = lead hook to the body, 8 = rear hook to the body, 9 = lead uppercut to the body, 10 = rear uppercut to the body. The head shots are 1-6, the body versions continue the count.
  • The "b" suffix. No 7-10 at all. Body shots are called 3b, 4b, 5b, 6b - a body hook is "three to the body." Common in gyms that find double-digit combos unwieldy to shout.
  • Local house systems. Some gyms add an overhand as 7, or number a lead body jab differently, or stop at 6 entirely. It's a coaching convenience, and coaches customise it.

Practical advice: drill 1-6 until they're automatic, and ask before you assume anything above 6. If you're following an app or a video that calls a 7, it should tell you what it means by it. If it doesn't, it's guessing that you share its convention.

The combinations worth learning first

Numbers are only useful because they chain. These five cover most of what a beginner needs, and they're the ones that show up in almost every class.

1-1-2Double jab, crossThe second jab closes distance the first one measured.
1-2-3Jab, cross, lead hookTwo straights bring the guard together, the hook goes around it.
1-2-5-2Jab, cross, lead uppercut, crossStraights, then up the middle, then straight again as they re-guard.
1-6-3-2Jab, rear uppercut, lead hook, crossAlternating angles - hard to defend because each punch arrives on a different line.

The principle underneath all of them: alternate the line of attack. Straight, then round, then up. Two punches on the same line are easy to block with one hand. Three punches on three lines are not. Common mistakes when drilling by number

  • Racing the count. Coaches call numbers fast; that doesn't mean throw them fast. Landing a clean 1-2 beats rushing a 1-2-3-2 that's four half-punches.
  • Dropping the off hand. Every number has a defensive half. The hand you're not punching with lives on your cheek.
  • Standing still. A combination thrown flat-footed is a combination you can't get out of. Finish with movement - a step, a pivot, a slip.
  • Learning 10 numbers before owning 2. A crisp 1-2 is worth more than a memorised list you throw sloppily.

Drilling this without a gym

The numbering system's real advantage is that it works with no equipment and no partner - someone calls numbers, you throw them. That's the entire mechanic of shadowboxing, and it's why calling combos out loud beats freestyling in a mirror: the call forces reaction time into the drill instead of letting you throw only what you're comfortable with.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the boxing punch numbers 1 to 6?

1 is the jab (lead hand straight), 2 is the cross (rear hand straight), 3 is the lead hook, 4 is the rear hook, 5 is the lead uppercut, and 6 is the rear uppercut. These six are consistent across almost every boxing gym. They describe lead and rear hands rather than left and right, so for a southpaw the 1 is a right jab and the 2 is a left cross.

What are punch numbers 7, 8, 9 and 10 in boxing?

There is no universal standard past 6. The most common convention repeats punches 3 through 6 as body shots: 7 is a lead hook to the body, 8 a rear hook to the body, 9 a lead uppercut to the body, and 10 a rear uppercut to the body. Many gyms skip 7-10 entirely and use a b suffix instead - a body hook becomes 3b. Some add an overhand or a house-specific punch. Ask your coach which system they use rather than assuming.

What does 1-2 mean in boxing?

A 1-2 is a jab followed immediately by a cross - the most fundamental combination in boxing. The jab occupies your opponent's guard and measures the distance, and the cross follows through the gap it creates. Almost every longer combination is built on top of a 1-2, which is why coaches drill it more than anything else.

Do I need to learn all ten numbers?

No. Learn 1 through 6 properly and you can throw the overwhelming majority of combinations used in real boxing. Beginners are consistently better served by a crisp jab and cross than by a memorised list of ten punches thrown sloppily. Add body shots once the head-level punches are automatic and your guard stays up while you throw them.

Scope

Technique descriptions here are a general reference, not coaching. Punch numbering is a gym convention rather than a standard, and yours may differ past number 6. If you are new to boxing, get a coach to watch your form before you drill anything heavily - repeating a bad hook a thousand times is worse than not drilling it at all.
