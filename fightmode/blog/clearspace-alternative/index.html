The 6 best Clearspace alternatives for iPhone

Clearspace built its reputation on a good idea: put something between the impulse and the app instead of just slamming a door. Breathing exercises, intention prompts, and in some versions a physical challenge before your feed loads. It works for a lot of people. It also loses a lot of people - usually to the subscription, or to the slow discovery that a pause you control is a pause you can skip. Here are six alternatives, sorted by how they stop you rather than how nice the onboarding looks.

Quick Answer

If you want Clearspace's core idea for free, iPhone Screen Time plus ScreenZen covers most of it at no cost. If the pause is what you liked but it stopped working, you need a harder gate - Brick's NFC tag or a locked-session tool like Freedom. And if your honest problem is that you skip past every pause you build, an exercise-to-unlock app like FightMode is the category that fixes it: the apps open when you finish a 60-second boxing round, and there is nothing to talk yourself out of.

Why people look for a Clearspace alternative

The reasons are consistent enough that they double as a shopping list. If one of these is you, it tells you exactly which replacement to look at:

  • The subscription. Paying monthly to be told to breathe is a hard sell after the novelty wears off, especially when free tools do a version of it.
  • The pause stops landing. Any friction you administer to yourself becomes background noise eventually. Month one it interrupts you. Month four you tap through it without reading.
  • It slows you down but doesn't stop you. A pause is designed for reflex opens. It was never built to stop a deliberate two-hour binge, and it mostly doesn't.
  • Nothing fills the hole. Blocking removes a habit without replacing it. The urge doesn't disappear - it goes shopping for a different app.

1. FightMode - the pause you can't skip

Clearspace asks you to sit with the urge for a few seconds. FightMode asks you to spend it. Your chosen apps stay locked, and the way through isn't a passcode or a countdown you can wait out - it's a 60-second boxing round with a coach calling combos and an AI scorecard grading whether you actually threw them.

That difference matters more than it sounds. A breathing screen is a request, and requests get declined. A physical toll is a transaction: throw the punches or stay out. There's no negotiation to lose, because there's no negotiation. And in practice, most people are 30 seconds into a round before they remember what they were going to open, at which point the urge has already passed on its own.

The side effect is the part that keeps people using it. Ten unlocks a day is ten minutes of real boxing work. Over a month that's conditioning you didn't schedule. See why boxing works better than pushups as friction.

2. iPhone Screen Time - the free baseline you already own

Before paying for anything, know what's already on the phone. App Limits caps daily minutes per app or category. Downtime blanks everything outside an allowed list on a schedule. For light use it's genuinely adequate, and it costs nothing.

The known failure is the "Ignore Limit" button. iOS politely offers to let you through, and after two weeks of tapping it the limit is decoration. Walkthrough in how to block apps on iPhone, and an honest head-to-head in Screen Time vs third-party blockers.

3. ScreenZen - the closest free match

If what you want is Clearspace without the bill, ScreenZen is the first thing to try. Configurable delays before an app opens, intention prompts that make you type why you're going in, and limits on how many times per day you can open something - with a free tier that covers the core.

It's the same philosophy, so it inherits the same ceiling: it nudges, it doesn't wall. If nudging is enough for you, you can stop reading here and save yourself a subscription. Full breakdown in the ScreenZen comparison.

4. one sec - the purest version of the pause

one sec strips the idea down to one move: tap the icon, get a few seconds of forced breathing, then get asked whether you still want in. No streaks, no dashboard, just the interruption.

It works far better than it should, because most phone opens are reflex rather than decision - interrupt the reflex and a large share of them evaporate. It struggles with the same thing Clearspace struggles with: if you genuinely intend to scroll for an hour, a breathing screen is a formality. See the one sec comparison.

5. Brick - distance instead of willpower

Brick is a small NFC tag you stick somewhere in your home. Tap to lock your apps; tap again to unlock. Walk out the door bricked and your phone stays boring until you come back.

It's the most physically honest option here, and it works for a reason no software can copy: you can't tap a tag that's in another building. The costs are that you're buying an object, you have to keep track of it, and forgetting it cuts both ways. Software alternatives to Brick chase the same effect without the purchase.

6. Freedom - if the phone isn't the whole problem

Clearspace is a phone story. Freedom's argument is that the distraction follows you to the laptop, so the block should sync across devices. Blocklists, scheduled sessions, and a locked mode that makes quitting early deliberately annoying.

If you lose as many hours to a browser tab as to an app, this is the more complete answer. If your problem is purely thumb-shaped, it's more tool than you need. See the Freedom comparison.

How to pick, in one paragraph

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there a free alternative to Clearspace?

Yes, and you should try the free options before paying for anything. iPhone's built-in Screen Time gives you App Limits and Downtime at no cost, ScreenZen offers configurable delays and intention prompts on a free tier that closely mirrors what Clearspace does, and FightMode's exercise-to-unlock blocking is free on the App Store. Run Screen Time for two weeks first - it will tell you whether your problem is reflex opening or deliberate binges, which is the thing that determines what you actually need.

Why do people stop using Clearspace?

The three most common reasons are the recurring cost, the pause losing its effect over time, and the gap between what a pause is designed for and what people need it for. A breathing prompt is built to interrupt reflex opens, and it does that well. It was never built to stop someone who has decided to scroll for an hour, and after a few months of tapping through it the prompt becomes background noise. None of that is unique to Clearspace - it is a structural limit of every pause-based tool.

What is the difference between a pause app and an app blocker?

A pause app lets you into the app after a short delay or prompt, betting that the delay kills the impulse. A blocker refuses entry entirely until some condition is met - a schedule ends, a tag is tapped, a task is completed. Pause apps are gentler and better for reflex scrolling; blockers are harder and better for deliberate binges. Most people start with a pause app, outgrow it, and only then discover which category they actually needed.

Can any iPhone app blocker be made impossible to bypass?

No, and be suspicious of any that claims otherwise. Apple does not let a consumer app make itself unremovable, so every blocker can ultimately be deleted or disabled. What the better tools do is make bypassing slow, effortful, or physically inconvenient enough that the impulse passes before you finish. Hardware gates like Brick and exercise gates like FightMode work on exactly this principle: the block isn't unbreakable, it's just more expensive than the scroll is worth.
