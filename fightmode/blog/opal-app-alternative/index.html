The 7 best Opal alternatives for iPhone

Opal made focus sessions feel premium - clean design, streaks, session stats. But plenty of people bounce off it: the subscription, the fact that a determined thumb can usually talk its way out of a session, or just that scheduling focus blocks never matched how their day actually goes. Here are seven alternatives, sorted by how they stop you rather than by how nice they look.

Quick Answer

The best Opal alternative depends on what you want to fix. For free scheduled blocking, iPhone's built-in Screen Time covers the basics. For a pause instead of a wall, try a friction app like one sec. For blocks that genuinely hold, look at hardware-gated options like Brick or a locked-mode blocker like Freedom. And if the real problem is that you always find a way around the block, an exercise-to-unlock app like FightMode is the category that fixes that - you don't disable it, you earn past it with a 60-second boxing round.

Why people look for an Opal alternative

Opal is a good app. The reasons people leave it are pretty consistent, and they tell you what to look for in a replacement:

  • Subscription fatigue. A recurring charge for a blocker feels different from a recurring charge for something you actively use. Blockers succeed by being invisible.
  • Sessions you can end. Any block with an escape hatch eventually becomes a block you escape. If your session ends whenever the urge is strong enough, you've bought a speed bump, not a wall.
  • Scheduling doesn't match real life. The 9am-noon focus block is great until the scroll urge hits at 9:15pm on a Tuesday.
  • Blocking without replacing. Removing the app leaves a hole. If nothing fills it, the urge just goes shopping for a different app.

Keep those four in mind and the list below sorts itself.

1. FightMode - the one you have to earn past

Every other app on this list is some version of "stop." FightMode is the only one built around "pay." Your chosen apps stay locked, and the way through isn't a passcode or a five-minute grace timer - it's a 60-second boxing round with a coach calling combos and an AI scorecard grading whether you actually threw them.

That changes the psychology completely. With a normal blocker, the block is a negotiation you have with yourself, and you win that negotiation more often than you'd like to admit. With a physical toll, there's nothing to negotiate with. Either you throw the punches or you don't get in. Most of the time, halfway through the round, you've forgotten what you were going to open.

2. iPhone Screen Time - the free baseline

Before you pay for anything, know what you already have. Screen Time gives you App Limits (a daily cap per app or category) and Downtime (a scheduled window where only allowed apps open). It's genuinely fine for light use, and it costs nothing.

The catch is the "Ignore Limit" button. When the limit hits, iOS politely offers to let you through, and after two weeks of tapping it, the limit is decoration. Full walkthrough in how to block apps on iPhone, and an honest comparison in Screen Time vs third-party blockers.

3. one sec - a pause, not a wall

one sec takes a different bet: don't block the app, just insert a deep breath between the impulse and the opening. You tap the icon, you get a few seconds of forced breathing, then you're asked whether you actually still want to go in.

It works far better than it sounds, because most phone opens are reflex, not decision. Interrupt the reflex and a large share of them evaporate. Where it struggles is the deliberate binge - if you genuinely want to scroll for an hour, a breathing screen won't stop you. See the full one sec comparison.

4. Brick - hardware you have to go touch

Brick is a small NFC tag you stick somewhere in your home. Tap it to lock your apps; to unlock, you have to physically walk back and tap it again. Leaving the house bricked means your phone stays boring until you get home.

It's the most physically honest solution here, and the reason it works is distance - you can't tap a tag that's in another building. The cost is that you're buying an object, you have to keep track of it, and if you forget it you're stuck either way. Software alternatives to Brick cover the same idea without the purchase.

5. Freedom - if your problem isn't only the phone

Opal is mostly a phone story. Freedom's pitch is that your distraction follows you to the laptop, so blocks should sync across devices. Blocklists, scheduled sessions, and a locked mode that makes ending a session deliberately painful.

If you lose hours to a browser as much as to an app, this is the more complete answer. If your problem is purely thumb-shaped, it's more tool than you need. See the Freedom comparison.

6. Pushscroll - exercise friction, one exercise

Pushscroll popularized the exercise-to-unlock idea: do pushups, get your apps. It's the right mechanism, and the fact that people search for alternatives constantly says something about how the specific implementation lands - pushups get old, and they're the same movement every time.

If the concept appeals but the exercise doesn't, read the Pushscroll alternative breakdown and the full category comparison.

7. ScreenZen - free and gentle

ScreenZen sits between Screen Time and one sec: configurable delays, intention prompts, and open limits, without a subscription. It's a reasonable free upgrade over the built-in tools if you want more control than Apple gives you and you respond well to a nudge rather than a wall.

How to pick, in one paragraph

Match the tool to your failure mode

You open apps without deciding to - a friction app (one sec, ScreenZen). The pause is the whole fix.

You keep disabling your blocker - an earn-it-back app (FightMode) or hardware (Brick). Willpower is not the variable to optimize.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best free alternative to Opal?

iPhone's built-in Screen Time is the best free starting point - App Limits and Downtime cover the core of what Opal does at no cost. Beyond that, ScreenZen and one sec both have usable free tiers, and FightMode's exercise-to-unlock blocking is free on the App Store. Start with Screen Time for two weeks to learn whether your problem is reflex opening or deliberate binges, then pick a paid tool only if the free one fails in a specific, identifiable way.

Why do people stop using Opal?

The three most common reasons are the subscription cost, sessions being endable when the urge is strong, and scheduling that doesn't match real life - the scroll urge rarely respects a calendar. None of these are unique to Opal; they're structural problems with schedule-and-block apps generally. If any of them describe you, look for a tool with a different mechanism rather than a nicer version of the same one.

Is there an app blocker you actually cannot bypass?

No consumer iPhone app can make itself impossible to remove - Apple doesn't allow that, and you should be suspicious of anything that claims otherwise. What the better tools do is make bypassing slow, effortful, or physically inconvenient enough that the impulse passes first. Hardware gates like Brick and exercise gates like FightMode work this way: the block isn't unbreakable, it's just more expensive than the scroll is worth.

What makes exercise-to-unlock different from a normal blocker?

A normal blocker asks you to not do something, which puts you in a willpower argument you will sometimes lose. An exercise-to-unlock app asks you to do something instead - the apps open after you complete a physical task. You're never negotiating about whether the block should stand, only about whether you feel like working. And unlike a delay timer, the time spent produces something: over a month it adds up to real conditioning.
