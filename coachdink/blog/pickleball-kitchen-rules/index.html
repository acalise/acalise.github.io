Pickleball Kitchen Rules (Non-Volley Zone) and How to Use the Line

The kitchen is the most misunderstood part of the pickleball court. Plenty of players believe you can never step in it. You can. The rule is narrower than that, and once you understand exactly what it forbids, the kitchen line stops being a place to be nervous about and becomes the best spot on the court.

The short answer

The kitchen (non-volley zone) is the 7-foot area on each side of the net. You may not volley (hit the ball out of the air) while touching the kitchen or its line, and if your momentum after a volley carries you into the kitchen, that is also a fault, even if the ball is already dead. You can stand in the kitchen and hit any ball that has bounced. After being in the kitchen, both feet must be back outside before you volley.

What and where the kitchen is

The kitchen, officially the non-volley zone, is the area on each side of the net that extends 7 feet back from the net, across the full width of the court. The line at the back of it, the kitchen line or non-volley line, is part of the kitchen. Touching the line counts the same as touching the zone.

The rule exists to stop players from standing at the net and smashing every ball out of the air. It is the reason pickleball has a soft game at all, and the reason dinks, drops and resets matter.

The kitchen rules, precisely

What is a fault

Volleying while touching the kitchen. If any part of you is touching the non-volley zone or its line when you hit a volley, it is a fault.

Momentum into the kitchen. If, after volleying, your momentum carries you into the kitchen or onto the line, it is a fault, even if the ball has already bounced twice or gone out on the other side.

Your stuff counts too. If your paddle, hat or anything you are wearing or carrying touches the kitchen as part of the volley, it is a fault.

What is allowed

Standing in the kitchen. You can enter and stand in the kitchen at any time.

Hitting a bounced ball in the kitchen. If the ball bounces in the kitchen, you can step in and hit it. Only volleys are restricted.

Volleying from outside with the paddle over the kitchen. Your paddle can be above the kitchen when you volley, as long as your body does not touch the zone or line.

One more detail catches people: if you have been in the kitchen, both feet need to be re-established outside it before you can volley. Hopping back with one foot and volleying immediately is a fault.

The momentum rule in practice

The momentum rule is where most kitchen faults happen at club level. A player lunges forward to volley a ball just over the net, makes the shot, and then stumbles onto the line. The volley is a fault even though it already happened, because the act of volleying is not considered finished until the player has regained balance.

The fix is mostly about where you stand. If you stand right on top of the line, any forward lunge takes you into the kitchen. Standing a few inches behind gives you room to reach without falling in, and keeping your weight balanced instead of leaning far forward helps you stop.

Why everyone wants to be at the kitchen line

In doubles, the team that gets both players to the kitchen line first usually has the advantage. From the line:

  • You can volley balls before they drop at your feet.
  • You are close enough to hit down on any ball that comes up above the net.
  • Opponents have less space to hit through you, because you cover more of the angles.

From the baseline, the opposite is true: balls land at your feet, you hit upward, and the opponents at the line attack. That is why staying stuck at the baseline is one of the most expensive habits in the game, and why the third shot drop exists.

Kitchen line strategy

  1. Get there and stay there. Once you are at the line, do not drift back after each shot. Backing off leaves the next dink landing at your feet.
  2. Stand just behind the line, not on it. A few inches of space prevents momentum faults and still lets you reach short balls.
  3. Paddle up. At the line, the ball can come at you fast. A paddle held at chest height in front of you is ready for both a dink and a speed-up.
  4. Dink patiently. Keep the ball low in the opponents' kitchen until you get one above the net. See dinking tips.
  5. Move with your partner. If one of you is pulled wide, the other shifts toward the middle. A gap in the middle is the easiest shot to hit through.
  6. Step into the kitchen only for bounced balls, and step out again right away so you are ready to volley the next one.

Common kitchen misunderstandings

MythReality
You can never go in the kitchen. You can, any time. You just cannot volley while touching it.
If the ball is dead, a step into the kitchen does not matter. If the step is momentum from your volley, it is still a fault.
Your paddle cannot cross over the kitchen. It can. Only your body and things touching the ground matter.
The kitchen line is safe to stand on. The line is part of the kitchen. Volleying with a foot on it is a fault.

The serve and the kitchen

The kitchen also matters on the serve: a serve that lands in the kitchen or on the kitchen line is a fault. That is covered in more detail in our serve rules and tips.

Advanced kitchen-adjacent shots: the Erne and the ATP

Two shots come up whenever people discuss the kitchen, because both look like they should break the rules but, done correctly, do not.

The Erne

An Erne is a volley hit from outside the court, beside the net post, after the player has moved there by jumping over the corner of the kitchen or by going around it outside the sideline. It is legal because the player never touches the kitchen. If they land in or touch the kitchen or its line before or during the volley, or while carried by momentum afterward, it is a fault. Ernes are usually set up by a dink that drifts toward the sideline.

The ATP (around the post)

An around-the-post shot is hit from outside the sideline and travels around the net post rather than over the net, landing in the opponents' court. It is legal and does not have to clear the net height. It is not a kitchen rule as such, but it usually happens off a wide dink, so it belongs in the same conversation.

Neither shot is something to build a game around. Both are fun, both can win points against opponents who dink wide, and both are much easier to fault on than they look.

Kitchen line footwork

Good kitchen line play is mostly small movements done well.

  • Shuffle, do not cross over. Moving side to side at the line, small shuffle steps keep you facing the net and balanced.
  • Step in, hit, step out. When a dink lands short in the kitchen, step in with one foot, play it, and step back out immediately. Then you are ready to volley the next ball.
  • Lean, do not lunge. For balls just out of reach, a lean from a wide, balanced stance keeps you out of the kitchen; a lunge often ends with a foot on the line.
  • Reset your base after every shot. Feet roughly shoulder-width apart, knees bent, paddle up.

Calling kitchen faults in recreational play

In most recreational games there is no referee, and players call their own kitchen faults. The general expectation is that you call your own fault when you know you touched the kitchen during a volley. Opponents can see your feet better than you can, but etiquette generally leaves the call with the player who committed it. If you are unsure whether you touched the line, a fixed video is one of the few ways to find out after the game.

What filming a game shows you

Players are usually surprised by how far back they really stand. It feels like you are at the line, but the video often shows a step or two behind it, or a slow drift backward over the course of a rally. A fixed camera from the corner of the court shows exactly where both players' feet are on every shot. Frequently asked questions

Can you step in the kitchen in pickleball?

Yes. You can enter and stand in the kitchen at any time. What you cannot do is volley the ball while touching the kitchen or its line, or let your momentum from a volley carry you into it.

Is the kitchen line part of the kitchen?

Yes. Touching the line counts as touching the non-volley zone. A volley hit with a foot on the line is a fault, and a serve that lands on it is a fault.

Can you hit a ball that bounces in the kitchen?

Yes. The kitchen only restricts volleys. If the ball bounces in the kitchen, you can step in and play it.

What is the momentum rule in pickleball?

If you volley from outside the kitchen and your momentum then carries you into the kitchen or onto its line, it is a fault, even if the ball is already dead. The volley is not complete until you have regained your balance.

How big is the pickleball kitchen?

It extends 7 feet from the net on each side and runs the full 20-foot width of the court.
