How to Serve Better in Pickleball: Technique, Rules and Faults

The serve is the only shot in pickleball you control completely. Nobody is hitting at you, nobody is moving, and you choose when to hit. That makes it the easiest shot to get reliable, and it makes every missed serve an unforced point given away.

The short answer

The best pickleball serve for most players is a deep, consistent serve that lands near the opponent's baseline, not a hard one that sometimes misses. Legally, you can use a volley serve (underhand, upward arc, contact below the waist) or a drop serve (drop the ball and hit it after it bounces). At least one foot must be behind the baseline and neither foot may touch the court or baseline at contact. A serve that lands on the kitchen line is a fault; one that lands on the sideline or baseline is in.

What a good serve actually does

Because of the two-bounce rule, the receiving team has to let the serve bounce, and the serving team has to let the return bounce. The serve itself is rarely a winner. What it can do is push the returner back so the return is shorter and weaker, which makes your third shot easier.

That is why depth matters more than pace. A deep serve keeps the returner behind the baseline, so they take longer to get to the kitchen line. A short serve lets them step in, hit an aggressive return, and walk straight up. Serves landing short are one of the most common habits that show up on video, because from the baseline it is genuinely hard to judge where your own ball landed.

The two legal serves

The volley serve

You hit the ball out of the air without letting it bounce. Under the USA Pickleball rules:

  • Your arm must be moving in an upward arc when you hit the ball.
  • Contact must be below your waist (the navel is the usual reference).
  • The head of the paddle must not be above the highest part of your wrist at contact.

The drop serve

You drop the ball from your hand and hit it after it bounces. The ball must be released, not tossed, thrown or bounced downward, and you can drop it from any height. Once the ball is dropped correctly, the volley serve's motion restrictions (upward arc, below the waist, paddle head position) do not apply.

The drop serve is popular with beginners and with players whose volley serve is inconsistent, because it removes timing from the equation: the ball bounces to the same height every time.

Check the current rulebook

Serve rules are among the most frequently revised parts of the official rulebook. The details above reflect the current USA Pickleball rules as we understand them, but if you play sanctioned events, check the latest version for any changes.

Where your feet must be

  • At least one foot must be behind the baseline, on the ground, when you hit the ball.
  • Neither foot may touch the court or the baseline at contact.
  • Your feet must be within the imaginary extensions of the centerline and the sideline of the court you are serving from.

Stepping on the baseline as you serve is the classic foot fault. It happens most when players try to add power by stepping into the serve. If you are foot faulting, start further back and keep the step shorter.

Is a serve on the kitchen line in or out?

Out. The kitchen line is part of the kitchen (non-volley zone), and a serve that lands in the kitchen, including on its line, is a fault. The other lines work the opposite way: a serve that lands on the sideline, the baseline or the centerline of the correct service court is in.

Common serve faults

  • Landing in the kitchen or on its line.
  • Landing in the wrong service court. The serve must go diagonally to the receiver's service court.
  • Out of bounds past the baseline or outside the sideline.
  • Into the net without going over.
  • Foot fault on or over the baseline, or outside the centerline/sideline extensions.
  • Illegal motion on a volley serve: contact above the waist or a sideways or downward swing.
  • Serving after the time limit. The server has a limited time (10 seconds under current rules) after the score is called.

A serve that clips the net and lands in the correct service court is in play under current rules; there is no let replay. You also get only one serve attempt per turn, unlike tennis.

How to serve better

  1. Pick one serve and groove it. A single reliable motion beats a menu of fancy serves. Spin and power can come later.
  2. Aim deep, with margin. Target a spot a few feet inside the baseline. Aiming at the baseline itself leads to serves sailing out.
  3. Use the same routine every time. Same stance, same bounce or hold, same breath. Routine is what makes serves consistent under pressure.
  4. Swing through the ball, not at it. A smooth low-to-high swing with a full follow-through gives depth without much effort.
  5. Serve to the backhand. For most returners, a deep serve to the backhand produces the weakest return.
  6. Recover after the serve. Stay behind the baseline and get ready for the return, which you must let bounce.

The return is half the serve story

When you are receiving, the same logic flips. Hit the return deep, then follow it in to the kitchen line. A short return gives the serving team an easy third shot, and not following the return in throws away the advantage the two-bounce rule gives the receiving team. Both habits are easy to see on video and hard to feel from the court.

Serve variations, once the basic serve is reliable

A consistent deep serve is the foundation. Once you rarely miss, a few variations can make returns harder, as long as you do not trade away consistency to get them.

  • Depth variation: mixing a very deep serve with one a little shorter but still past the middle of the service court can catch returners who have settled into one spot.
  • Direction variation: serving wide pulls the returner off the court and opens space; serving down the middle reduces their angles.
  • Pace variation: a slower, higher deep serve can be harder to return aggressively than a fast flat one, especially for players who like pace to work with.
  • Spin: topspin serves dip and kick, while slice serves skid and move sideways. Spin rules have changed over time, particularly around imparting spin with the hand on the drop serve, so check the current rulebook before relying on spin you create with your fingers.

The test for any variation is simple: does it produce weaker returns without producing more missed serves? If it costs you serves, it is not worth it yet.

Calling the score and the serving order

In doubles, the server calls the score before serving, as three numbers: the serving team's score, the receiving team's score, and the server number (1 or 2). The first server for a team serves from the right side when their team's score is even and from the left when it is odd. Getting the server and side right matters, because serving from the wrong court or with the wrong server is a fault. If you play rally scoring formats or singles, the calling rules differ, so check what your event or club uses.

Serving under pressure

Missed serves cluster at the worst moments: game point, a close score, or just after an argument over a line call. A routine helps more than any technical tip here. Take the same number of breaths, bounce the ball the same number of times, pick the same target, and swing with the same smooth tempo. The goal is that the serve at 10-10 feels exactly like the serve at 0-0. If you still feel tight, aim a little further inside the lines; a serve that lands safely in the middle of the service court is far better than a fault.

What filming a game shows you

From the baseline you cannot see how deep your serve landed, and nobody tells you. A corner-of-court video shows every serve's landing spot over a whole game, which is far more honest than memory. Frequently asked questions

Is a pickleball serve on the kitchen line in or out?

Out. A serve that lands on the kitchen (non-volley zone) line is a fault. A serve that lands on the baseline, sideline or centerline of the correct service court is in.

What is a foot fault on a pickleball serve?

Touching the baseline or the court inside it with either foot at the moment of contact, or having your feet outside the imaginary extensions of the centerline and sideline. At least one foot must be on the ground behind the baseline.

Is the drop serve legal in pickleball?

Yes. You can drop the ball from your hand and hit it after it bounces. The ball must be dropped, not tossed or thrown, and the volley serve's motion rules do not apply.

Can a pickleball serve hit the net?

Yes. Under current rules, a serve that touches the net and lands in the correct service court is in play. There are no let serves.

What is the best serve for beginners?

A deep, consistent serve with a simple motion. Many beginners find the drop serve easiest because the ball bounces to the same height each time.
