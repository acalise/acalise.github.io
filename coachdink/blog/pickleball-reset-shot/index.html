The Pickleball Reset Shot: How and When to Hit It

A reset is the shot you hit when you are in trouble. The opponents are attacking, the ball is coming hard and low, and you are not where you want to be. Instead of hitting back harder, you take the pace off the ball and drop it softly into the kitchen. The rally goes back to neutral, and you get to keep playing.

The short answer

A reset is a soft shot hit off a fast or attacking ball that lands in the opponents' kitchen, turning their attack back into a dink rally. Get low, hold the paddle out in front with a loose grip, and absorb the ball with a short block or push rather than swinging. The difference from a drop: a drop is a soft shot off a slow ball from deep in the court, while a reset is a soft shot off a fast ball, usually from the transition zone or at the line.

Drop vs reset

The two shots have the same goal (land the ball softly in the kitchen) and are often confused. The difference is the ball you are hitting.

DropReset
Incoming ball Slow, has bounced (often the return) Fast, often a drive or speed-up, sometimes volleyed
Where you are Usually near the baseline Transition zone or at the kitchen line
Motion Short lift from low to high Absorb and redirect, very little swing
Purpose Get from the baseline to the line Survive an attack and neutralise it

In practice, a reset from mid-court off a hard drive is the shot that keeps your team in points you would otherwise lose. The third shot drop gets you moving forward; the reset rescues you when the move forward gets interrupted.

Why resets matter so much

Getting from the baseline to the kitchen line rarely happens in one shot. Most of the time you hit a drop, take a few steps, and the opponents hit a ball at your feet while you are in the transition zone, the area between the baseline and the kitchen. That is where points are won and lost at most levels. Players who can reset from there keep moving forward. Players who cannot either pop the ball up or try to drive their way out, and both usually end the point.

How to hit a reset

  1. Stop and split step. As the opponent swings, land balanced on both feet. You cannot reset well while moving.
  2. Get low. Most balls you need to reset are low. Bend your knees so the paddle can stay under the ball without pointing at the ground.
  3. Paddle out in front. Meet the ball early. Contact beside or behind your body loses control.
  4. Loose grip. This is the single biggest factor. A relaxed grip absorbs the ball's pace; a tight grip sends it back as fast as it came.
  5. Little or no backswing. Think block, not swing. The ball already has the energy; your job is to take it away.
  6. Slightly open face, short push. Just enough lift to clear the net and drop into the kitchen.

The feel you are after

A good reset feels like catching the ball on the paddle and setting it down over the net. If you can hear a loud pop at contact, you are probably gripping too hard or swinging too much.

Reset tips that make the biggest difference

  • Aim for the middle of the kitchen, not the net tape. You need margin, especially off a fast ball.
  • Prefer resetting to the player who is not attacking, or cross-court, where you have more net and more court.
  • Accept a slightly high reset over a netted one. A reset that lands short of the kitchen but low is still often good enough. A reset in the net ends the point immediately.
  • Do not reset and stay. Once you have neutralised the ball, keep moving toward the line on the next shot.
  • Do not reset everything. If a ball comes up high, that is a ball to attack, not one to take pace off.

Resetting at the kitchen line

Resets are not only for the transition zone. When an opponent speeds up at you during a dink rally, you can block the ball softly back into the kitchen instead of counterattacking. This is often the better choice when the speed-up is aimed at your body or low, because a hard counter from an awkward position frequently ends in the net. The same principles apply: loose grip, paddle in front, minimal swing.

Common reset mistakes

  • Popping up balls at your feet. Usually a stiff grip or reaching down with the paddle instead of bending.
  • Swinging at fast balls. Adding pace to an already fast ball sends it long or into the net.
  • Stalling in the transition zone. Standing still mid-court after a reset invites the next ball at your feet. Reset, then move forward when the ball allows.
  • Moving while hitting. If you are still running when the ball arrives, your paddle angle changes every time. Split step first.

How to practise resets

One player stands at the kitchen line and drives or speeds up balls at a partner standing in the transition zone. The partner's only job is to land each ball in the kitchen. Start with moderate pace and build up. Once the resets are consistent, add movement: after each successful reset, the partner takes a step forward, until they reach the line. That combines the reset with the transition, which is how it happens in a game.

Against a wall, stand a few steps back and hit firm balls, then try to absorb the rebound and send it softly back. It is hard at first and very good for grip pressure.

Resetting balls at different heights

Not every ball you need to reset arrives at the same height, and each one needs a slightly different paddle position.

  • Balls at your feet: the hardest reset. Bend deeply at the knees, keep the paddle head up rather than pointing at the ground, and use an open face to lift the ball just enough. Popping these up is the most common way points end in the transition zone.
  • Balls at knee to waist height: the most comfortable reset. A short block with a slightly open face, aimed at the middle of the kitchen.
  • Balls at your body: often hit on the backhand, with the paddle held across the body. Many players find the backhand block the most reliable reset in this position, because the paddle face is easier to control.
  • Balls above the net: usually not a reset at all. If you are balanced and the ball is above the net, attack it instead.

Forehand or backhand?

For fast balls in front of the body, many players find the backhand reset more stable, because the paddle can stay out in front with less wrist movement. This is a common reason coaches encourage players to hold the paddle slightly toward the backhand side in the ready position. Whichever side you use, the principles are the same: paddle in front, loose grip, minimal swing.

The mental side of the reset

The reset is a shot of restraint. When a hard ball comes at you, the instinct is to hit it back harder. That works occasionally, and it is memorable when it does, but most of the time a hard counter from a defensive position goes into the net or back to an opponent who is waiting for it. Choosing to take pace off the ball, and accept a neutral rally instead of trying to win the point immediately, is a decision you have to make before the ball arrives.

A useful way to think about it: the reset is not giving up the point, it is resetting the point to zero. From a neutral dink rally, you have a fair chance again. From a desperate counter, you mostly do not.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a drop and a reset in pickleball?

Both land softly in the kitchen. A drop is hit off a slow, bounced ball, usually from near the baseline, to help you move forward. A reset is hit off a fast or attacking ball, usually from the transition zone or at the line, to neutralise the opponent's attack.

How do I stop my resets from popping up?

Loosen your grip, get lower with your legs, and keep the paddle out in front with very little swing. A tight grip and a big swing are the usual causes.

Where should a reset land?

In the opponents' kitchen, ideally toward the middle of it for margin. A reset that is low over the net but lands slightly past the kitchen is often still good enough.

Should I reset or counterattack a speed-up?

If the ball is low or aimed at your body and you are not balanced, reset it. If it is high enough and you are set, a counter can work. Resetting is the higher-percentage choice for most players.
