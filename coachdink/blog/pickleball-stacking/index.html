Pickleball Stacking Explained: How to Stack, and When It Is Worth It

Stacking looks confusing the first time you see it: both players crowd onto one side of the court before the serve, then one of them dashes across as the point starts. It is not a trick or a loophole. It is a simple way for a doubles team to keep each player on the side of the court they prefer, regardless of the score.

The short answer

Stacking is a doubles positioning technique where a team lines up so that, once the point starts, each player ends up on their preferred side, usually to keep both forehands in the middle or a stronger player on a particular side. It is legal because the rules only require the correct player to serve or receive from the correct court. Where players stand otherwise, and where they move once the ball is in play, is up to them.

Why the normal rotation causes a problem

In standard doubles, players switch sides each time their team wins a point on serve. That means over the course of a game each player spends a lot of time on whichever side the score puts them, not the side that suits them. For a team with one right-hander and one left-hander, or one player with a much stronger forehand, that rotation regularly puts the team in its weaker formation.

Stacking fixes that. The team still serves and receives from the correct positions, as the rules require, but arranges itself so that one player immediately moves to their preferred side as soon as the ball is in play.

Why stacking is legal

The rules care about two things at the start of a point:

  • The correct player serves, from the correct service court, based on the score.
  • The correct player receives the serve, from the correct court.

Beyond that, partners can stand anywhere on their side of the court, including outside the court lines, and can move anywhere once the ball is served. Stacking uses exactly that freedom. The usual mistake is not stacking itself but losing track of who should serve or receive, which is a fault.

What teams are trying to achieve

Common reasons to stack

Two forehands in the middle. A right-hander and a left-hander can stack so the right-hander plays the left side and the left-hander plays the right, putting both forehands in the middle of the court.

Stronger player on one side. Some teams want the player with the better forehand on the left so they can take more middle balls.

Hiding a weakness. A player with a weak backhand can be kept on the side where fewer balls come to their backhand.

How to stack when serving

Serving is the simpler half because the serving team gets to start both players at the baseline.

  1. The server stands in the correct service court for the score, as normal.
  2. The partner stands right next to the server, on the same side of the court, slightly off the court or behind the server.
  3. After the serve, the partner moves across to the other side, and the server stays put or slides over as needed so each ends up on their preferred side.

If the server is already on their preferred side for that point, no switch is needed; the partner simply stands on their normal side.

How to stack when receiving

Receiving is harder because the returner has to hit the return, then cross the court while running to the kitchen line.

  1. The returner stands in the correct receiving court for the score.
  2. The partner stands at the kitchen line on the same side, usually just outside the sideline so they do not block the returner.
  3. After hitting the return, the returner runs diagonally to the far side of the kitchen line, while the partner slides over to their preferred side.

This is where stacking gets risky. The returner's path is longer, and if the return is short the opponents can hit into the gap before the switch is finished. A deep return buys the time the switch needs.

The half stack

Many club teams use a half stack: they stack only on the serve, or only when the server's partner is on the wrong side, and play standard positions when receiving. It gives most of the benefit with less risk and less to remember. For teams new to stacking, this is the easiest place to start.

Keeping track: signals and scorekeeping

The main danger of stacking is losing track of who should serve or receive. Some habits help:

  • Know who started serving. The player who served first for your team in the game serves from the right side when your score is even, and from the left when it is odd. Remember that, and you can always work out the correct server.
  • Agree on the switch before every point. Many teams use a simple hand signal behind the back on the return: open hand for switch, fist for stay.
  • Call the score out loud and check it. Mistakes usually start with a score that nobody confirmed.

Is stacking worth it for you?

Stacking makes the most sense for regular partners with a clear reason to prefer specific sides, such as a left-right pair. For players who rotate partners at open play, the extra coordination often costs more than it gains. The fundamentals in our doubles strategy guide (getting to the line together, moving as a pair, covering the middle) matter more than formation, and a stack does not fix a team that is not doing those.

A worked example

Imagine a team with a right-handed player, Alex, and a left-handed player, Sam. They want Alex on the left side and Sam on the right, so both forehands are in the middle. Alex served first for the team this game.

  • Score even, Alex serving: Alex must serve from the right. Sam stands beside Alex on the right side, slightly behind or off the court. After the serve, Alex slides across to the left and Sam moves into the right side.
  • Alex wins the point, score now odd: Alex serves again, this time from the left. That is already Alex's preferred side, so Sam simply stands on the right. No switch needed.
  • Receiving, Sam is due to receive on the left: Sam receives from the left, with Alex at the kitchen line on the same side. After returning, Sam runs across to the right side of the kitchen line, and Alex slides left.

Written out, it looks like a lot. On court, most teams settle into it after a few games, because there are only two questions each point: who must serve or receive from where, and do we need to switch?

Common stacking mistakes

  • Wrong server or receiver. The only real rules risk. Track the first server and the score.
  • Blocking the returner. The partner standing at the kitchen line on the returner's side should be outside the sideline or clearly out of the way.
  • Short returns while switching. A short return gives opponents an easy shot into the space the returner is running across. Return deep when you plan to switch.
  • Missed signals. One player switches, the other stays, and both end up on the same side. Make signals simple and confirm them.
  • Stacking without a reason. If both players are equally comfortable on either side, stacking adds risk without benefit.

What opponents do against a stacking team

Experienced opponents sometimes target the switch. On the return, they may hit their third shot toward the side the returner is running away from, or into the middle while both players are moving. Against a team stacking on serve, they may return wide to the side the server's partner is leaving. The counter is the same in every case: deep returns and serves to buy time, and a willingness to skip the switch on a point when the ball does not allow it. Staying where you are for one shot is always better than switching into a ball you cannot reach.

What filming a game shows you

The weak point of a stack is the moment of the switch, and that is exactly the part neither player can see during the point. A corner-of-court video shows both players' paths at once: whether the returner got across before the third shot, and whether the gap in the middle opened while you were swapping. Frequently asked questions

Is stacking legal in pickleball?

Yes. The rules only require the correct player to serve and the correct player to receive, each from the correct court. Partners can stand anywhere else on their side and move anywhere once the ball is served.

Why do pickleball teams stack?

To keep each player on their preferred side regardless of the score, most often to put both forehands in the middle for a left-handed and right-handed pair, or to keep the stronger player on a particular side.

What is a half stack in pickleball?

Stacking only some of the time, typically when serving, while playing standard positions when receiving. It gives much of the benefit with less risk.

How do you keep track of who serves when stacking?

Remember which player served first for your team in the game. That player serves from the right when your team's score is even and from the left when it is odd.
